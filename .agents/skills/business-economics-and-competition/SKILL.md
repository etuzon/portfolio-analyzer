# Business Economics And Competition

Define the actual product market, customers, geography, substitutes, and business
model before choosing peers or describing market share.

- Decompose revenue into price, volume, mix, FX, and acquisitions only where
  disclosures support the split. Growth alone does not prove pricing power.
- Separate gross, contribution, operating, and incremental margins. Define which
  costs are variable, fixed, recurring, or allocated.
- Align cohorts and periods for unit economics. Separate acquisition cost,
  retention, churn, payback, and lifetime-value assumptions; avoid a precise LTV
  from short or unstable retention history.
- Support switching costs, network effects, scale economies, or brand claims with
  observable evidence. A popular product does not establish a moat.
- Test substitutes, entrants, bargaining power, capacity, regulation, and market
  concentration. Define the denominator for market share; an industry label alone
  is not a comparable product market.
- Distinguish backlog, bookings, contracted/recurring revenue, and cash receipts.
  Read cancellation, conversion, and recognition conditions.
- Map major customer/supplier, regional, labor, energy, transport, and financing
  dependencies. Keep disclosed exposure separate from estimated exposure.
- Examine offsets: a price rise can improve unit margins while reducing demand;
  cheaper inputs can benefit competitors and erode selling prices.

Return supported drivers, competitor comparisons, leading indicators, and revenue/
margin scenario assumptions. Use `$company-report-industry-outlook` for statements
and `$equity-valuation-and-financial-modeling` for value. Do not turn qualitative
advantages into arbitrary score points or causal percentages.
